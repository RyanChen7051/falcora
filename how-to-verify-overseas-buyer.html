How to verify an overseas buyer before you pay

How do you verify an overseas buyer before the deposit leaves your account? Not by reading their website and hoping. This guide sets out five checks that leave you with evidence you can open, date and hand to a colleague — or to your finance team — before anyone signs.

Finding a buyer is not the same as verifying one. Lead lists are easy to produce and easy to fake. The expensive mistake happens later: a deposit wired to an entity that looked convincing on a profile page. Verification is a separate job, and it should be done before the conversation turns commercial.

The rule that makes the rest work

Never let one source stand in for another, and never let a missing value be filled in. A directory entry, a website and a tender notice answer different questions. If one comes back empty, you record empty — you do not borrow an answer from the others.

1. Confirm the legal entity exists

Start with the exact name. Cross-border deals die on small differences: a trading name versus a registered name, a local-language spelling, a subsidiary presented as the parent, or a company that changed hands. Record the name as it appears in each public source, the country, and any publicly listed registration identifier you can actually open.

What to write down: the source URL, the exact string you found, and the date you retrieved it. If a source lists the company under a different name, record both rather than silently deciding they are the same entity. The decision to equate two names is yours, and it should be visible in the file.

2. Confirm the domain is really theirs

A working website proves that a website works. It does not prove that the company behind it is trading, nor that the brand still controls the domain. Three checks that cost minutes:

  • Open the root domain and follow redirects. Note the final landing page. A domain that now resolves to a parked page, an unrelated marketplace or a holding-company portal is a materially different signal from an active product site.
  • Record the exact protocol that opens. Do not construct a URL by assuming https. Store the URL that actually worked, so a reviewer can reproduce your result.
  • Treat status codes carefully. A 200 response is not proof of reachability, and a 403 or 429 usually means bot protection rather than a dead business. A local filtering or hijack page is a separate state again. A site that does not respond is not automatically defunct — it may be blocking automated access. Label the state, do not guess the cause.

3. Find independent evidence of operation

This is the step most teams skip, and the one that carries the most weight. Look for the company in public records that were not written by the company itself:

  • Trade show exhibitor records — an appearance at a named exhibition, with the year.
  • Association member directories — a public membership listing maintained by an industry body.
  • Public tender notices — procurement records where the organisation appears as a buyer.

Two cautions. First, a directory source link often points to the directory home page, not to the company row; a reviewer may need to search the company name again. Say so explicitly in your notes rather than pretending the link opens the exact record. Second, suppliers and buyers are different things. An exhibitor list is a list of suppliers; a tender notice is buyer-side evidence. Mixing them into one claim is how a plausible-looking report becomes a wrong one.

For a fuller treatment of what each source type can and cannot prove, see how to verify a company with public sources.

4. Verify corporate contacts, role-based only

Keep three layers separate:

L1 · Public company informationName, country, website, publicly listed basics.
L2 · Corporate contactsRole-based addresses and phone numbers only, each with its own source link and retrieval date.
L3 · Personal contactsUser-supplied only. The platform never collects, infers or displays them.

Check that a role address belongs to a function (sales@, purchasing@, info@) rather than a named individual, and that it sits on the company's own domain. A phone number should pass a format check against the country dialling code rather than being accepted because it looks right. Never promote a personal address found elsewhere into L2, and never invent one to complete a field.

5. Record it as a three-state evidence pack

The deliverable is not a verdict. It is a file where every line can be traced back to something a colleague can open. Each item gets exactly one of three states:

FoundValue present, with a clickable public source and a retrieval date.
Found but emptyThe source was reached and contains no value. Recorded as empty, not back-filled.
Not foundNo result. Recorded as not found, with no guessed substitute.

There is deliberately no fourth state called no risk. A clean-looking file is not a guarantee, and no public-source check can produce one. The judgement stays with the person deciding whether to sign.

Red flags worth pausing on

A domain that changed hands recently; a contact address on a free mail provider; a request to move the conversation off the company domain; pressure to wire a deposit before any verification; a company that appears in only one source; and any report that fills in a blank with a confident-looking value. None of these proves fraud. All of them deserve a written question in the file.

Common questions

How do I verify an overseas buyer before wiring a deposit?

Work through five checks: confirm the legal entity exists, confirm the domain really belongs to it, look for independent operating evidence in trade show exhibitor records, association member directories or public tender notices, verify only role-based corporate contacts, and record every line as a three-state result with a clickable public source and a retrieval date.

What is the fastest way to check if a foreign company is legitimate?

Start with independent sources rather than the company's own website. A single directory entry is weak evidence; two or three unrelated public sources that name the same entity are much stronger. Always note what each source actually proves, because a name in a directory is not proof of current trading activity.

Can a company website alone prove that a buyer is real?

No. A live website only proves that a website exists. HTTP 200 is not the same as a reachable, active business, and a real domain may no longer be controlled by the brand you think it belongs to. Check where redirects land and record the exact protocol that opens, then corroborate with independent directories.

What should I do when a source returns no result?

Label it as not found and stop. Do not back-fill the gap with a plausible value, another directory or a guessed email. We use three states: found, found but empty, and not found. An honest empty field is far more useful in a deal review than a confident-looking guess.

Does FALCORA provide personal contact details or a risk score?

No. We list only publicly listed role-based corporate contacts, each with its own source link and retrieval date. Personal contacts are user-supplied only and are never collected, inferred or displayed by the platform. We do not produce a risk score and never output a no-risk conclusion; the judgement stays with you.
